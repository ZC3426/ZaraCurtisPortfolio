Business Customer Portal Redesign

Rebuilding self-service for gas and power customers.

Role
Product Designer (end-to-end)
Timeline
3 months
Team
Project Manager, Engineer, QA
Status
Launching in Late October TBC; impact metrics to follow
Scope
17 pages plus a design system, and a first iteration of a Managing Agent portal (deprioritised)
Tools
Claude Code, Figma, Git, Jira, Miro, Confluence, the company community hub
Results so far

Self-service that testers could actually use

15% → 100%testers who could submit a meter reading, before vs after iteration
95%of testers found all usage information in 3 seconds or less
WCAGaudit completed, with fixes to colour contrast and text size, and legal requirements built into the final designs
Problem

An unreliable portal that pushed customers to the phone

Now showing: Dashboard

The old portal was used by only around 30 customers a month. It technically let people do everything they needed, but the site often crashed, invoices and documents were hard to find, and meter readings regularly failed to send. Most customers phoned customer service instead. Most calls were about meter reads, VAT queries and invoice queries.

Our goal was to let customers help themselves, so the company wouldn't need to keep hiring in metering, customer service and credit as it grew.

Research

Who we designed for

Our primary customers were businesses buying gas and power directly, not through a broker. I started from customer service call data, which showed what they needed most: submitting readings, tracking usage, viewing site information, changing tenancy, requesting VAT changes, paying invoices and downloading documents. The PM and I then met stakeholders to learn what they wanted, which industry terms to keep, and which were internal-only and would confuse users.

Managing Agents and brokers have much larger portfolios, so I designed a first iteration of a Managing Agent portal. It was then deprioritised to focus on direct customers, and the design system and layouts were built so it can be picked up later.

Constraints

Dense data, a short timeline and low trust

Dense data

Sites, meter reads, invoices and usage all needed a lot of information on screen.

Three months

Development started early: the engineer began building before my designs were finished, so I worked in priority order and stayed in close contact.

Stakeholder trust

The business had been through several product and delivery teams in recent years. I couldn't test with real customers yet, so I relied on stakeholders and customer service, who handle customer queries every day.

Process

How we worked

The PM built rough functional prototypes in Claude to show what each page needed to do, so stakeholders could react to something working instead of a document. They were good for agreeing functionality but not for usability. My job was to turn them into something people could actually use, and I built and refined every design in Claude Code.

I used AI to generate code and layout options, and made the decisions myself: what to build, what to test, and what to throw away. The versions are:

Testing & iteration

Making dense data readable

This was the hardest part of the project. I tested each page in rounds of 5 people, and whenever 3 or more struggled with a task, I went back and iterated. In total I tested with 30 people from across the business, from HR to Credit Control. Most had never used the portal, which gave me honest usability data.

Earlier rounds ran on working prototypes, so testers could really enter readings and pay invoices, not just react to screens. I also tested all functionality on the live host the engineer built, using live data, so I could check how pages worked and where the built UI differed from my designs.

Invoices

Customers typically have a few accounts with a few sites each, so the first version used a drop-down table. Stakeholders wanted the VAT question visible outside the table, while the team wanted it inside, where users already see VAT. We included both. VAT queries were one of the most common reasons for calling, which is why its placement mattered.

Testing showed that users didn't realise rows could expand, got stuck on outstanding invoices when trying to view all, and struggled to pay selected invoices.

What I changed: I turned rows into cards so they looked clickable, moved outstanding invoices into their own simple table, and renamed the main button "Pay all" to encourage full payment, a key business requirement. I kept a separate payment button so customers in credit can still pay.

Result: 95% of testers answered the key questions.

Meter readings

The first version was also a drop-down table, and it confused users in the same way. I had to cover long meters, day and night registers, and corrected meters.

I avoided a full table at first because I thought it would look cluttered. But our customers understood meter readings far better than I did, and they entered readings quickly in a full table. So I went with that.

Stakeholders were against designing for mobile at first. I advocated for it for three reasons. Customers use the portal out on their sites, so they need to submit readings remotely, standing at the meter rather than back at a desk. A phone's camera lets them photograph the meter instead of copying a long string of digits by hand, so fewer readings come in wrong. And WCAG's reflow guideline expects pages to work at narrow widths without sideways scrolling, which also helps anyone zoomed in on a desktop.

Result: at first only 15% of testers could submit readings. After iterating, 100% could.

Usage

The original scattered data across the page with no clear message about how usage was going.

I combined cost vs forecast and target into one card and turned top sites into a pie chart. I removed sites needing attention, because it wasn't actionable here and the dashboard already surfaces it. I made the gas/power toggle and site selector sticky, and added a toggle between energy units and pounds so users can connect usage to cost.

Result: 95% of testers found all the information in 3 seconds or less.

Sites

Sites had to work grouped by account or individually, and stakeholders wanted everything visible.

A table wasn't an option after the earlier testing, so I used cards.

Some feedback suggested users identify sites by account, so I added a group-by option.

Individual site pages have a sticky header so users always know where they are. Customers can also now do things that used to mean a phone call: report a change of tenancy, add site contacts, add property and site references, add site-level data to improve usage predictions, and view past meter readings. I expect these, together with clearer meter reading and invoice flows, to take pressure off the team.

Documents, Support, Consolidated Billing, Settings

These were more straightforward: grouping information logically and applying the design system so they matched the rest. Documents moved from a card-based page to a searchable, filterable list with counts, grouped by time period, so customers with many sites can find a specific letter quickly.

Dashboard

The page with the most direction changes

The dashboard started with upsells, but stakeholders didn't want any, so I removed them and added the information they asked for. They then decided the existing design system, created by the designer before me, didn't suit the brand, and asked for a new dashboard. With the freedom to leave that system behind, I matched the company website, using an edited version of its new design system, and produced two versions.

Design system

Extending an inherited system, in code

I inherited a design system from the designer before me. It was bland, so I extended it with more colour, new responsive components and new visuals. I started by maintaining the existing system in Figma, then moved to iterating on it myself in code, using Git, Claude and a repo hosted on the company's community hub. That hub meant the PM, engineer and stakeholders all worked from the same components. The engineer took the code and the designs, which is why handoff could start while I was still designing. The dashboard is the exception: it uses a modified version of the website's system.

Adding colour

Stakeholder feedback was that the pages felt too similar and plain. I added a colourful header and footer and redesigned the cards, so each page has its own look while staying one product.

The final version: matching the new website

Near the end of the project, stakeholders asked for the portal to match the new company website, which was launching at the same time. They hadn't wanted that before, so I redesigned the system to match it for the final version.

Delivery

Shipping it, and what comes next

Accessibility & compliance

I audited the portal against WCAG and fixed issues with colour contrast and text size, among others, and worked with legal to build the required additions into the final designs.

Handoff & QA

Engineering handoff began before my designs were finished, because development moved so fast. QA happened at the end of the project, and I supported it by checking the built pages against my designs.

Measuring impact

After launch I'll track monthly logins, self-service meter reading submissions, and call and email volume, then update this case study with real numbers.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

One unified prototype

Each page was built as a separate prototype and wired together later. One shared prototype would have been easier to test and hand over.

Design system first

I built it alongside the pages, reactively. Building it first would have saved rework, especially when brand direction changed.

Stakeholders earlier

Brand direction changed late, and earlier involvement would have caught it sooner.
