Business Customer Portal Redesign

Turning a self-service portal that customers avoided into one they can rely on, so routine tasks don't need a phone call.

Role
Product Designer. Sole designer from V2 onwards, building on the PM's V1 prototypes and an inherited design system.
Timeline
3 months, 2026
Team
PM, engineer, QA. Worked with customer service, metering, credit control and legal.
Status
Launching late October 2026. Live impact data to follow.
Scope
17 pages, an extended design system and a new one matched to the new company website. A Managing Agent portal was designed, then paused.
Tools
Figma, Claude Code, Git, Jira, Miro, Confluence
Problem
Only around 30 business customers used the portal each month. Most phoned instead, mainly about meter readings, VAT and invoices.
What I did
Used call data, journey mapping and stakeholder sessions to find the tasks that mattered, redesigned 17 pages around them, and tested with 30 colleagues and 4 customers.
Result so far
In pre-launch testing, meter reading completion went from 15% to 100%, and answers to key invoice questions from 40% to 95%. Every page tested met the pass bar.

Problem

An unreliable portal that pushed customers to the phone

Now showing: Dashboard

The old portal technically let customers do everything they needed. In practice it often crashed, invoices and documents were hard to find, and meter readings regularly failed to send. Only around 30 customers a month used it.

So most customers phoned instead. The top reasons were meter readings, VAT and invoices. Much of this was failure demand: contact caused by the service not working, not by customers needing to speak to someone.

The business needed to grow without hiring at the same rate in metering, customer service and credit control. Customers needed to get routine tasks done without waiting on the phone. A portal that worked would serve both.

The wider service

The portal is one part of a bigger service

The portal is the last part of a much longer journey. Before a business customer ever logs in, their contract has passed through brokers, contract services, account managers, credit risk, trading, metering and billing.

So I mapped the whole gas and power customer journey, front and back stage, from a broker's tender to billing and renewal. I built the maps from an account manager walkthrough and checked them against how the sales systems actually behave, which added rework loops, statuses and teams the first version had missed.

Mapping it also showed upstream problems that customers would otherwise meet in the portal. For example, documents were filed under the customer's name, so after a change of tenancy older documents could no longer be found.

I then looked at each page in terms of that journey: what the customer is trying to do, which team handles it behind the scenes, and where it was breaking down.

Research

Who we designed for, and how we found out

Users. Our primary users were businesses buying gas and power directly, not through a broker. Most have a few accounts, each with a few sites.

Methods.

  • Call data analysis. Customer service data showed what customers needed most: submitting meter readings, tracking usage, viewing site information, changing tenancy, requesting VAT changes, paying invoices and downloading documents.
  • Stakeholder sessions. The PM and I met customer service, metering, credit control and legal to understand their goals and constraints.
  • Customer journey mapping. I mapped the whole gas and power customer journey, from a broker's tender to billing and renewal, so I could see where the portal fits within it (see The wider service).
  • Language review. We agreed which industry terms customers know and should keep, and which were internal jargon that would confuse them.
  • Usability testing. Task-based testing on working prototypes (see Testing).

Managing agents and brokers manage much larger portfolios, so I designed a first version of a separate Managing Agent portal. It was paused when priorities changed, and I built the design system and layouts so it can be picked up later.

Constraints

Dense data, a short timeline and low trust

Dense data

Sites, meter readings, invoices and usage all needed a lot of information on screen.

Three months

Build ran alongside design, so I designed in priority order and worked closely with the engineer to keep the two in step.

Earning confidence, limited customer access

New work needed to earn stakeholders' confidence quickly. Access to customers was limited, so I tested mostly with staff who deal with customer queries every day, then with 4 customers at the end.

Process

How we worked

The PM built rough functional prototypes in Claude so stakeholders could react to something working rather than a document. They were useful for agreeing what each page should do, but not for usability. From V2 onwards, I was the designer, working with the PM: I turned those prototypes into something people could use, and built and refined every design in Claude Code.

Testing & iteration

Making dense data readable

This was the hardest part of the project. Participants did real tasks on working prototypes: submitting a meter reading, finding and paying an invoice, answering a VAT question, checking usage against their contract.

I tested each page in rounds of 5. If fewer than 4 in 5 people completed a task, I reviewed it, and if only 2 in 5 or fewer did, I redesigned it before moving on. In total I tested with 30 people from across the business, from HR to credit control.

Testing mostly with staff has limits, so I used the mix deliberately. People who had never used the portal showed whether it was easy to learn. Metering and customer service staff checked it matched how the service really works. At the end, I tested with 4 real customers, who completed everything they set out to do. I also tested every feature on the engineer's live build with live data, to catch where the build differed from my designs.

Billing and invoices

Customers typically have a few accounts with a few sites each, so the first version used an expandable table.

VAT queries were one of the most common reasons for calling, so where the VAT option sat mattered. Stakeholders wanted it visible above the table; the team wanted it inside, next to the VAT figures. In practice, customers used both, depending on when they noticed the VAT on their account, so we kept both: a clear route for anyone arriving with a VAT question, and one in context for anyone who spots it while checking an invoice.

Testing showed that users didn't realise rows could expand, got stuck on outstanding invoices when trying to view all, and struggled to pay selected invoices.

What I changed: I turned rows into cards so they looked clickable, and moved outstanding invoices into their own simple table. I made "Pay all" the main action, because clearing the full balance was a key business need and the simplest route for most customers. I kept a separate "Make a payment" option so customers who need to pay a different amount aren't forced down one route.

Result: the share of participants who answered the key invoice questions rose from 40% to 95%. The ease rating rose from 4 to 8.5 out of 10.

Meter readings

The first version was also an expandable table, and it confused users in the same way. I had to cover long meters, day and night registers (separate readings for different times of day), and corrected meters.

I avoided a full table at first because I thought it would look cluttered. But it tested better: it made the fields people expected to see highly visible, and it followed a clear left-to-right order for entering a reading. So I went with it.

I also linked a guide on how to read and submit meters. Meter readings were a big source of calls for the customer service team, because the people submitting them, such as treasurers, didn't always take the readings themselves.

Mobile wasn't in scope at first. I made the case for it for three reasons. Customers use the portal out on their sites, so they need to submit readings remotely, standing at the meter rather than back at a desk. A phone's camera lets them photograph the meter instead of copying a long string of digits by hand, so fewer readings come in wrong. And WCAG's reflow guideline expects pages to work at narrow widths without sideways scrolling, which also helps anyone zoomed in on a desktop. Fewer wrong readings also means fewer estimated or corrected bills, and fewer calls later in the journey.

I tested the mobile designs on both iPhone and Android, to make sure every page worked across operating systems, not just on one device.

Result: at first only 15% of participants could submit meter readings. After iterating, 100% could. At first, participants hadn't finished entering their readings after 15 seconds; by the final round, everyone finished in under 8. The ease rating rose from 2 to 9 out of 10.

Usage

The original scattered data across the page with no clear message about how usage was going.

I combined cost vs forecast and target into one card, and turned top sites into a pie chart. I removed sites needing attention, because it wasn't actionable here and the dashboard already surfaces it.

I made the gas and power toggle and the site selector sticky. That way, people looking at the heat map, the site comparison table or targets further down the page can still switch between sites and views without scrolling back to the top. I also added a toggle between energy units and pounds, so users can connect their usage to what it costs.

Result: 90% of participants found all the information within 3 seconds, in every round.

Sites

Sites had to work grouped by account or individually, and stakeholders wanted everything visible.

Testing on invoices showed that expandable tables hid information, so I started with cards.

Some feedback suggested users identify sites by account, so I added a group-by option.

Individual site pages have a sticky header so users always know where they are. Customers can also now do things that used to mean a phone call: report a change of tenancy, add site contacts, add property and site references, add site-level data to improve usage predictions, and view past meter readings. These move tasks from phone to self-service. After launch, I'll track whether calls about them go down.

Documents, support, settings and sign-in

These were more straightforward: grouping information logically and applying the design system so they matched the rest. Documents moved from a card-based page to a searchable, filterable list with counts, grouped by time period, so customers with many sites can find a specific letter quickly.

Dashboard

Designing through changing direction

The dashboard changed direction more than any other page. It started with upsells, which stakeholders then removed, so I replaced them with the information they asked for. Later, stakeholders decided the inherited design system didn't suit the brand and asked for a new dashboard. I used that as a chance to match the company website, adapting its new design system, and produced two versions.

Not every direction survived. Stakeholders rejected the themed dashboard below because they felt it didn't match the company's current brand image. The final dashboard opens the walkthrough at the top of this page.

The final dashboard

For the final dashboard I explored three header ideas: an illustrated skyline, a map of the UK, and a map of the customer's own sites. Below the header, the cards answer the questions customers ask most: is anything due, are my meter readings sent, and what is my usage.

Design system

Two design systems: one extended, one new

I worked with two design systems on this project. I extended the one I inherited, then replaced it with a new one when the brand direction changed.

1. Extending the inherited system

The system I inherited from the designer before me was built mostly from greys. It worked, but the pages looked plain and too alike. Rather than start again, I extended it.

I added more colour, new responsive components and new visuals, including a colourful header and footer and redesigned cards, so each page has its own look while staying one product.

I started by maintaining the system in Figma, then moved to iterating on it in code, using Git, Claude and a shared repository the PM, engineer and stakeholders all used, so everyone worked from the same components. The engineer took the code and the designs, which is why handoff could start while I was still designing.

2. A new system to match the new website

Near the end of the project, stakeholders asked for the portal to match the new company website, which a contractor was still building. That was a new direction, and extending the old system was no longer enough. I designed a new system, with more rounded edges and curves, to match a website that was itself still in development.

Delivery

Shipping it, and what comes next

Accessibility & compliance

I audited the portal against WCAG and fixed issues with colour contrast and text size, among others, and worked with legal to build the required additions into the final designs.

Handoff & QA

Handoff ran page by page alongside design, to keep pace with development. QA happened at the end of the project, and I supported it by checking the built pages against my designs.

Measuring impact

After launch I'll measure:

  • Digital take-up. Monthly active customers, against a baseline of around 30.
  • Completion rate. Meter readings and payments completed online.
  • Reading accuracy. Fewer rejected or estimated readings.
  • Contact volume by reason. Calls and emails about meter readings, VAT and invoices.
  • Satisfaction. A short feedback prompt after key tasks.

I'll update this case study with real numbers.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

One unified prototype

Each page was built as a separate prototype and wired together later. One shared prototype would have been easier to test and hand over.

Design system first

I built it alongside the pages, reactively. Building it first would have saved rework, especially when brand direction changed.
