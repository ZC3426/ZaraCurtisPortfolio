Using Telematics to Analyse the Impact of Social Context on Driver Behaviour

Project type
UX Research / Behavioural Research
Role
Lead Researcher — behavioural analysis, insight synthesis, UX concept & intervention design
Duration
9 months
Context
University of Exeter
Methods
Naturalistic data analysis, survey design, mixed-effects modelling
Tools
Telematics app (DriveScore), Qualtrics, R
Problem

Designing for safer driving through social context

129,000+
total casualties from risky driving in the UK
~30,000
face death or serious injury

Many existing interventions rely on assumptions about who takes risks (e.g. "young drivers") rather than how social context actually shapes behaviour. Much of the existing evidence also relies on self-reports, crash statistics, or simulations, which don't reflect everyday driving.

Goal: understand how social context (passengers), age, and sex influence real-world driving behaviour, and translate those insights into design opportunities for safer driving experiences.

Research goals

  • Identify how risky driving behaviours vary by age and sex.
  • Understand how the presence of passengers changes driving behaviour.
  • Test whether common assumptions about "young risky drivers" hold up in real-world data.
Method

Passive telematics over self-report

To prioritise ecological validity, I used passive telematics data instead of self-reports. A smartphone telematics app automatically recorded speed, harsh braking, acceleration, harsh cornering, phone distraction, and speeding, alongside a daily contextual survey capturing passenger presence, passenger age/sex, and trip purpose.

33
UK drivers, aged 19–69
~14 days
of real-world driving data per participant

I analysed the data with Linear Mixed Models (changes in speed) and Generalised Linear Mixed Models (frequency of risky events), controlling for distance, driving experience, and repeated measures.

Hypotheses

H1 — Younger drivers are riskier

Based on crash statistics and lower driving experience predicting higher risk-taking and sensation-seeking. UX relevance: many insurance and safety products implicitly categorise younger users as "high risk" — this tested whether that holds.

H2 — Passengers increase risk for male drivers

Prior research suggests social signalling and peer presence amplify speed increases and competitive driving in male drivers. UX relevance: in-car feedback could adapt when passengers are present.

H3 — Passengers increase risk for younger drivers

Crash and simulation data often show higher collision risk for young drivers with passengers, underpinning policies like graduated driver licensing. This tested whether age-based restrictions align with actual behaviour.

Prior evidence often comes from observational studies (where participants know they're monitored), simulations (where there's no real risk), or crash data (which misses risks that don't result in crashes) — important limitations when designing real interventions.

Findings

The data challenged the assumptions

H1 & H3: not proven

Risky events actually increased with age, and passengers didn't increase risk for young drivers — contrary to expectations. Possible causes: younger drivers are more likely to be on telematics-based insurance that financially penalises risky driving, and Gen Z may simply have a lower overall appetite for risk.

H2: proven

Male drivers drove faster with more passengers; female drivers drove slower with more passengers — a pattern that held across age groups.

UX & design insights

  • Design systems change behaviour. Many young drivers are already subject to telematics-based insurance monitoring, meaning design was already shaping behaviour — just not always transparently.
  • Behaviour change isn't equal. The same situation (having a passenger) produced opposite behaviours by gender — a strong signal that one-size-fits-all safety design doesn't work.
  • People optimise around what's measured. Speed changed with passengers, but recorded "risky events" didn't always follow — raising the question: are we measuring safety, or training users to game the system?
The problem reframed: not "how do we stop risky drivers?" but "how might we design safety systems that account for social pressure, behavioural incentives, and human adaptation, without relying on fear or punishment?"
Limitations & next steps

What I'd explore next

  • The relatively small sample size may have limited the ability to detect real differences, particularly for H1 and H3.
  • Participants may have adjusted their natural behaviour as they got used to being passively monitored and logging their day.
  • If monitoring salience genuinely improves young drivers' behaviour, an app for new/learner drivers built around that principle could be worth exploring.
  • If drivers are avoiding punishment rather than driving safely for its own sake, they may still take risks they can "get away with" — future design should target underlying motivation, not just penalty-avoidance.
  • Since men account for the vast majority of driving deaths and injuries, understanding why passengers affect male and female drivers so differently deserves further study.
Reflections

Risk as a product of systems, not traits

I thoroughly enjoyed researching, planning, and executing this project. The real behavioural data showed that risk is often a product of systems, incentives, and social context rather than individual traits. Recognising this shifted my thinking from designing "corrective" solutions toward designing supportive, context-aware interventions that meet users where they are — understanding the underlying mechanisms at play, rather than trying to "fix" the user, to build systems that genuinely improve people's lives.
